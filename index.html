Where the Greeks Fought

Every recorded land and sea battle of the Greek city-states and kingdoms, from the first battles known from the sources, c. 700 BCE, to the last Greek kingdoms: where each was fought, against whom, and who won. With the win–draw–loss record of every state or people that fought at least five.

How it was made

What counts as a battle

A pitched land or sea battle, attested in an ancient source, with a Greek state as a principal belligerent. Sieges are left out unless a distinct battle was fought during them (a sortie, a relief battle, a harbour battle), and so are skirmishes, raids, ambushes of foragers and captures of a city without a fight. Mythical and legendary battles are left out (the Trojan War, the Seven against Thebes, the First Messenian War); archaic battles known only from late sources are kept where modern scholarship broadly treats them as historical, with the doubt in the note.

Who counts as Greek

Greek city-states, leagues and tyrannies anywhere: Greece, Asia Minor, Sicily and Italy, Massalia, Cyrene, the Black Sea and Cyprus. Macedon and Epirus. The Hellenistic kingdoms: Seleucid, Ptolemaic, Antigonid, Lysimachus, Attalid Pergamon, Greco-Bactria, the Indo-Greeks and the Bosporan kingdom. And, as Hellenized kingdoms, Pontus, Bithynia, Cappadocia and Commagene, so that the Mithridatic Wars count. Battles in which Greeks were only mercenaries or minor contingents of a non-Greek power are left out.

Colours and dots

Each battle takes the colour of its non-Greek principal belligerent, on either side: Romans, European peoples, Asian and African peoples, Persians and Lydians, or Carthaginians (where there are several: Romans first, then Persians, Carthaginians, Asian and African, European). A battle with no non-Greek principal is Greek v Greek, blue. Greek allies of a non-Greek power do not change the colour: Plataea in 479 is a battle against the Persians. Each place is drawn as a small pie: its area grows with the number of battles there, and the slices give the number against each opponent, starting at 12 o’clock and running clockwise in the order of the legend. A slice can hide under a neighbouring dot on a crowded map; zoom in to see it.

Wins, draws and losses

The victor is the side that won on the battlefield: it held the field, routed the enemy or destroyed the fleet. Strategic outcomes and disagreements between the sources are in the notes. A draw is an indecisive battle, or one that both sides claimed with no clear verdict from modern scholarship. Only principal belligerents are counted: the leading states and the allies the sources treat as major. Minor contingents are listed with each battle but not counted.

Research

Ten research batches by period and theatre, each following the same written rules and citing the ancient sources for every battle, then an independent fact-check of every row in five passes. The fact-check corrected results, dates and positions (several sea battles had been placed on land, and land battles at sea), removed entries that were not battles, and added battles that had been missed. Positions are approximate for most battles: 250 can be placed only within their region, and only 31 sites are known exactly.
